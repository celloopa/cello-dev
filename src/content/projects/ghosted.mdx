## The Problem

Job applications are repetitive, high-stakes and personal. You run the same tailoring loop dozens of times, and one invented claim can cost you the interview. An AI can make the loop much faster, but only if you can see exactly what it did to your resume and undo any of it.

That's the question Ghosted keeps answering: how much should the model do, and how does the person stay in control while it does it?

## How I Got Here: Versions One And Two

**Version one** was a Go terminal app. I wanted to feel the product's constraints directly, so I built it instead of only sketching it. Applications moved through an eight-stage pipeline stored in plain JSON. Agents could parse postings and draft documents, but nothing touched the tracker without a dry run or my approval.

It proved the workflow. It couldn't prove the product, because nobody else could use it.

**Version two** was a Next.js web app built on one rule: deterministic code does everything it can, the model only writes prose, and code checks the model. Code parsed the posting, scored fit from keyword coverage, and planned the changes. One bounded model call returned the letter, rewrites and answers. Then code checked them: a word cap, a banned-phrase list, and a "source not in CV" flag on any claim it couldn't trace. The core and the app had 744 tests between them.

It worked, but testers found it too complicated, and it had a gap I kept running into. The generated material lived in one place, while the resume, the thing that actually matters, lived somewhere else.

## The Product: A Resume You Can Talk To

In October 2026 I rebuilt Ghosted around one surface: a live resume canvas with a chat beside it. You ask for a change, and the change shows up on the page in yellow.

<MediaFull
  src="/images/projects/ghosted/v3/proposal.webp"
  alt="A job workspace for a Product Designer, Design Systems role at Northwind Labs. The chat on the left shows the request 'Lead the summary with design systems, and tighten the Asheville Dispensary bullets' and the reply listing what changed. On the right, the live resume has the new summary and four bullets highlighted in yellow under a bar reading 'AI proposal awaiting your decision' with Reject and Keep buttons"
  caption="Every AI change is a proposal: highlighted on the real page, waiting for Keep or Reject. (Demo data: my resume, a fictional job.)"
/>

- **A live canvas.** The resume is drawn in the browser by Typst, and the PDF comes from the same compiler, so what you see is what you download. An edit re-renders in about 7 ms.
- **Proposals, not edits.** Every change the agent makes is highlighted and saved as a version: who made it, when, and why. Keep it or Reject it. Undo and Restore only move a pointer, so nothing is ever deleted.
- **Evidence, not invention.** The base resume is a master copy that's never trimmed. When the agent needs a stronger bullet, it asks for specifics and writes only from what you told it. Each job gets a one-page version cut from it.
- **Three levels: base, archetype, job.** Jobs are filed into your own job families. Once a family has a few jobs, Ghosted can build an archetype resume for it, and new jobs in that family start from there.
- **Intake from the source.** A pasted job link is read through the hiring platform's own API first (Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, Workday and others) and trimmed to the job itself.

The [launch post](/blog/programmatic-resumes-you-can-chat-with) walks through the idea screen by screen.

## Process And Decisions

I decide; agents build. That split only works if the decisions are written down before the code, so the repo carries them: use cases with dated decisions, proposals for anything bigger than a day, ADRs for the things that set direction, and a release log where every production release records what shipped, why, and where the decision lives. That log is the source for this page.

For the bigger calls I get grilled. An agent asks me question after question until the edge cases are settled, and the answers go into the proposal. Two-way sync between the base resume and its copies came out of one of those sessions. Every release follows the same checklist: typecheck and a production build, check whether a tester is mid-session, snapshot the database, then ship.

A few decisions that show how I think about it:

- **One style, set from the base.** There's one style for the whole workspace, so a Style button inside a job was quietly restyling every resume. I moved Style to the base resume and archetypes, where the panel can say plainly that it applies to all your resumes.
- **Looks are presets, not layouts.** When Sam asked for templates, I made five looks of the one ATS-safe template rather than new two-column layouts. Sidebars and columns look nice and break applicant-tracking systems.
- **Accept became Keep.** "Accept" sounds like signing something. "Keep" is what you're doing: keeping a change, or not.
- **New conversation instead of clearing the chat.** Each request re-sent the last 20–40 messages. Now the AI only sees what comes after a "New conversation" divider, and the resume, posting, notes and letter always come along. Nothing gets deleted, so History links and requests keep working.

## Testing With Real Testers

Version three went live on October 8. The first tester used it that day, and her job notes were open in an interview the next morning. From then on, testers set the order of work. Here's what they said and what changed.

- **Jacky couldn't load her resume.** Her PDF had gone from Google Docs through "Microsoft Print to PDF", so the letters were drawn as shapes with no text to extract, and the app failed silently. Picture-only PDFs are now read with OCR (about 4 seconds for three pages), and a failed upload says why, right under the button.
- **Kim's test call** produced the longest list:
  - The page break looked like a spacing bug, so each page is now its own sheet labeled "Page 1 of 2".
  - She wanted to know when messages were sent, so chat has timestamps.
  - A half-typed message vanished when she switched jobs. Drafts now survive switching and reloading.
  - The job agent added an Operations skills group that never reached her base resume, so the next job started without it. Her idea was that the job "sends a request" to the base. That became two-way sync: copies notice when their source changed and can bring the change in, and new facts from a job chat go to the base as requests you can apply or decline.
  - She dismissed the new "your base changed" bar and lost the way back. Dismiss became "Not now", with a "Base updated" button that reopens it, plus "Choose what to bring in", which lists the changes in the chat and lets you pick.
  - She asked for formatted job notes. They shipped that evening.
  - She proposed naming downloads by role, since "it's obviously for the company you're applying to." Downloads are now "First Last — Role.pdf".
- **Sam asked for templates** after a demo, ones that apply with the live reload. That became Looks: five presets drawn from your own resume, applied live, with "Save as a look" for your own fine-tuning.
- **I tested it on my phone** and wrote down "we're getting a bit crowded." That became the focus pass (below).
- **Two of three new testers stopped before their first resume.** They signed in and never saved anything. That might just be timing. I wrote a first-run guide as a proposal and am holding it until I know whether the first step was unclear or they just didn't have time.

## Identity And Motion

By October 9 the product worked and still looked like a placeholder: an emoji for a logo and the system font. A design agent built a brand lab in Figma, with logo studies, a palette, type and a motion board. Claude reviewed it, and I made the calls.

<MediaFull
  src="/images/projects/ghosted/brand/logo-explorations.webp"
  alt="Four logo studies, each beside the lowercase wordmark 'ghosted': 01 Paper Ghost, a ghost with a folded page corner; 02 Quiet Companion, a plain ghost; 03 Back-and-forth, two interlocking corners; 04 The Editor, a lowercase g. Each is shown at 16, 24 and 32 px"
  caption="The logo studies. Only the Paper Ghost explains both the name and the product: a ghost made from a document."
/>

The insight was that the one ownable thing was already in the product: the yellow highlight that marks what the AI changed. So the highlighter yellow, `#FFE58A`, became the brand color. It only ever means the logo or a pending AI change, always as a background under ink, never as text, and it's the same in both themes. Blue stays for buttons and links. If the primary button were yellow, yellow would mean both "click here" and "the AI changed this".

- **The mark:** the Paper Ghost, simplified until the folded corner is the only detail, so it holds at 16 px. In the app it's just the ghost shape in ink.
- **Type:** DM Sans for all app UI and no monospace, so the serif resume reads as the document.
- **Rejected:** yellow buttons, a 3D ghost mascot (it clashed with the flat mark), and serif headings in the app (they compete with the resume).

<MediaTwo items={[
  { src: "/images/projects/ghosted/brand/paper-ghost.webp", alt: "The refined Paper Ghost mark beside the 'ghosted' wordmark and the line 'Your applications. Your next move.', with the mark at 16, 24, 32 and 64 px", caption: "The refined mark. The fold does the talking." },
  { src: "/images/projects/ghosted/brand/motion-moments.webp", alt: "Motion spec titled 'A change becomes yours': yellow proposal, your decision, plain ink, with timings for nine moments from the agent working to a board status change", caption: "The motion spec: nine moments, 100–220 ms, reduced motion for each." }

Motion only goes where it explains a change. The hero moment: when you keep a proposal, the yellow fades into plain ink in 180 ms while the text stays perfectly still. When a proposal lands, the highlights swipe in left to right behind a small Paper Ghost cursor. My first build ran the swipe in 280 ms, and testing it, I couldn't actually see the cursor. It now takes about 600 ms, the one deliberate exception to the 220 ms ceiling. The loader is the mark, and it only appears if a wait passes 200 ms. With reduced motion on, every one of these goes direct.

<MediaFull
  src="/images/projects/ghosted/v3/swipe-keep-full.webm"
  caption="A proposal lands and the ghost swipes the highlights in. Keep settles the yellow into ink, and the ghost gloats a little."
/>

The voice follows the same rule. Ghosted's chat is a calm editor with a little dry humor, and it never jokes about rejection, the job search, or errors. Resume text, letters and answers stay in the person's own voice, with no ghost references. Keep gets a short toast: what changed, plus one line like "Called it." or "Not bad for a ghost." That reversed an earlier "no jokes" call after I tried it, and I'm watching whether it gets old. The Discord kit has emoji for saved, applied, interviewing and offer, plus Keep and Reject for voting on ideas. There's deliberately no "rejected" emoji: testers are people actually getting ghosted.

## The Focus Pass

Testing on my phone, I wrote: "we're getting a bit crowded… keep the functionality but clean up the view so it's more relaxing and allows you to focus on the current task." So I measured it: 62% of the first phone screen was chrome before you saw any of the resume.

The rule became: show what the current task needs, keep everything else one tap away, and remove nothing. The job header became one line. Editing a job moved into the Posting tab. Older chat replies show their buttons only on hover or tap. The resume toolbar shrank to the few things you use. On phones, the resume now starts **233 px** down the first screen instead of **522 px**. Board cards got a "Move to" menu, because drag never worked on phones at all.

## By The Numbers

- **25** production releases on October 8–9, **19** of them on October 9.
- **522 → 233 px:** where the resume starts on a phone, before and after the focus pass.
- **62%** of the first phone screen was chrome before that pass.
- **880 → 30 ms** to open Style.
- **~7 ms** to re-render the resume after an edit.
- **Same evening:** Kim's formatted-notes idea, from request to production.
- **0** usage claims. Ghosted is days old with a handful of testers, and measuring how they actually use it is the next piece of work.

## How It Was Built

I'm direct about this part because it's part of the design. The flow, the chat-plus-canvas model, the choice to render with Typst, the brand, and every product decision above are mine, written down before any code. Claude built version three's first working cut in a day from that spec, and most of the releases since, while I tested, decided and reviewed. That's useful evidence of how I work with AI tools. It isn't a claim that I hand-wrote every line. The foundations I'm now taking over by hand are the resume data model, the template, the prompts and version history.

## What I Learned

Adding AI to a product is easy. The design work is deciding how much the model gets to do, and making sure the person can see, judge and undo every bit of it. Version three added a second lesson: the fastest way to find out what that means is to put it in front of real people on day one and let what they say set the order of work.
