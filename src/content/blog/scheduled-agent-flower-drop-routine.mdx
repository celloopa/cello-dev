If your team posts the same kind of product story every week, how much of that week goes to remaking it? For Asheville Dispensary's flower drops it was seven tools and a few hours, so I automated the routine and spent the time on better stories.

So far: animated stories the CEO liked, built so the next batch shouldn't add an editing pass. The new renderer still needs to be connected to the weekly drops, so everything below is the review cut, made with historical products and offers.

When I improve the template, the next run gets the improvement without a manual step. I said I'd use this style for the next drop.

## What did the routine take?

Seven tools and a few hours every week, by my rough count, mostly repeating decisions I'd already made.

I lead design for Asheville Dispensary and its related brands. An announcement arrived with the strains, tiers and sale wording. I found the product pages and gathered photos and videos. In Figma, I duplicated the story card and replaced its content. Then conversion commands, Premiere and Media Encoder put footage behind the card, masked it into a circle and exported the story. After that came the handoff: attach the files to Asana, post them for approval in Slack, and make sure the whole batch was there.

The layout was approved, the crop was known, the output size was known. I was carrying those decisions between applications.

## Did it need a video editor?

No. An old export turned out to be a card over product footage, with no animated artwork, and code can compose that.

I measured the mask and output settings, then composed the video with FFmpeg. That caught something easy to miss: exports meant to be silent could still carry an audio track. The generated version enforces silence.

## What went wrong?

Two things: a lookalike strain and a wrong tier. The routine had to read the announcement, find product media, fill the approved design, render the files and prepare the review handoff.

Search once returned a lookalike strain, and a stale sitemap pointed at an old page. I added direct URL checks, title matching and a record of why each page was picked.

Later, the announced tier disagreed with a website category, and the wrong tier was used. That set a clearer boundary: the announcement owns the offer and tier, and the website supplies the media.

I also separated preparing files from notifying people. Files attach quietly while a batch is completed. The review message waits for the whole batch and the right hours.

A dependable loop had to come first. Only then could I spend the recovered time on what it should produce.

## How do you keep a motion revision?

Save it as settings. “A little slower” is useful in conversation, but it doesn't preserve a revision.

The Study Lab in Storybook exposes timing and travel, so I can change one thing, replay it and inspect an exact frame. A saved study records when the name starts, how long it moves, when the sale stamp appears and how far things travel.

I checked the exported files and transitions against the preview too. A good-looking Storybook canvas wasn't proof that every deliverable worked.

## What changed in the stories?

The familiar card stayed, and the product got room to move. Of the layouts I reviewed, Familiar was my favorite, so I kept its card, circular footage and product identity.

The background flower cutouts got larger and now enter and exit with each slide, with positions and rotations varied across the batch. I zoomed the footage further for the two Private Reserve strains and left the Smalls wider, where the group of smaller buds is part of the image.

Then the motion went through every element: heading, name panel, pills, video window, seal and background cutout. Exits mattered as much as entrances. The next story needs a clean place to begin.

Last, I raised the heading and pills and moved the video up, slightly larger. More of the background flower shows at the bottom, and the product feels less boxed in.

## How does the end card fit the brand?

It reuses the leaf artwork from the brand audit and existing assets: a black card with light type for the logo and website, and no new decorative language just for this video.

That took a few revisions. A visible square in the leaf treatment interrupted the background, so I changed the crop and mask. Then the leaves got too subtle in the encoded version, so I brought them back until they showed in the actual file.

The last product story fades to black before the end card arrives. I checked the joins in the exported sequence, and playback at desktop and phone sizes. A transition only counts if it survives the export people will watch.

## What does it still cost?

Review, exceptions, occasional template changes and rendering time. I haven't timed a live batch with this version yet. The aim is to avoid an extra editing pass for each drop, without claiming the work has no ongoing cost.

I sent the team one combined preview, with the individual files alongside, so they could watch the pacing and review the pieces that would actually be delivered.

The earlier routine taught me to watch that cost. Idle days once got expensive because scheduled checks opened full AI sessions. Moving the first check into a script brought an idle day down to cents. That figure belongs to the old version.

## The style is set, and the renderer is next

The style is set for the next flower drop. Next, the new renderer gets connected, so the same website media feeds the animated templates and the same review receives the output.
