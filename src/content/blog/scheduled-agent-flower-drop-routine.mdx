Flower drops at Asheville Dispensary now have animated stories, and making the next batch shouldn't add an editing pass. Automating the old routine is what paid for the redesign.

The new renderer still needs to be connected to the live workflow. Everything below is the review cut, made with historical products and offers.

The design constraint that matters is already in place: improving the template changes what the next run produces, without adding a manual step to that run.

## What did the routine take?

I lead design for Asheville Dispensary and its related brands. Flower drops were a small, recurring job that filled more of the week than their size suggested.

An announcement arrived with the strains, tiers and sale wording. I found the product pages and gathered photos and videos. In Figma, I duplicated the story card and replaced its content. Then conversion commands, Premiere and Media Encoder put footage behind the card, masked it into a circle and exported the story. After that came the handoff: attach the files to Asana, post them for approval in Slack, and make sure the whole batch was there.

Seven tools and a few hours every week, by my rough count. Most of it repeated decisions I'd already made. The layout was approved, the crop was known, the output size was known. I was carrying those decisions between applications.

## Rebuild the approved card in code

My first goal was to get the existing work out reliably.

An old export turned out to be a short composition with no animated artwork: a card over product footage. Reproducing that didn't need a video editor.

I measured the mask and output settings, then composed the video with FFmpeg. That caught something easy to miss: exports meant to be silent could still carry an audio track. The generated version enforces silence.

## Fix the wrong strain and the wrong tier

The wider routine read the announcement, found product media, filled the approved design, rendered the files and prepared the review handoff. That took more than a rendering command.

Search once returned a lookalike strain, and a stale sitemap pointed at an old page. I added direct URL checks, title matching and a record of why each page was picked.

Later, the announced tier disagreed with a website category, and the wrong tier was used. That set a clearer boundary: the announcement owns the offer and tier, and the website supplies the media.

I also separated preparing files from notifying people. Files attach quietly while a batch is completed. The review message waits for the whole batch and the right hours.

A dependable loop had to come first. Only then could I spend the recovered time on what it should produce.

The old automation reproduced the old result. Now I wanted more variation: a clearer entrance, more room for the product, and a sequence that felt composed from start to finish.

Gentle rise, staggered slide and stamp pop let me compare how the name and sale treatment arrive.

The Study Lab exposes timing and travel, so I can change one thing, replay it and inspect an exact frame. “A little slower” is useful in conversation, but it doesn't preserve a revision. A saved study records when the name starts, how long it moves, when the sale stamp appears and how far things travel.

The browser preview and the offline export use the same frame-drawing function, and export reports include sampled frames to check the video against the preview. The later Familiar sequence had its own renderer, so I checked its encoded files and transitions directly too. I wasn't treating a good-looking Storybook canvas as proof that every deliverable worked.

## Give the familiar layout room to move

<MediaFull
  src="/images/blog/product-drops/familiar-before-after.webp"
  alt="Before and after comparison of the Familiar composition showing larger background flower imagery and a closer crop of the product footage"
  caption="One of the intermediate comparisons: more room for the background flower and a closer view inside the video window. Later revisions raised and enlarged the video again."
  uncropped

Of the layouts I reviewed, Familiar was my favorite. I kept the recognizable card, circular footage and product identity, and gave them more room to move.

The background flower cutouts got larger and now enter and exit with each slide, with positions and rotations varied across the batch. I zoomed the footage further for the two Private Reserve strains and left the Smalls wider, where the group of smaller buds is part of the image.

Then the motion went through every element: heading, name panel, pills, video window, seal and background cutout. Exits mattered as much as entrances. The next story needs a clean place to begin.

Last, I raised the heading and pills and moved the video up, slightly larger. More of the background flower shows at the bottom, and the product feels less boxed in.

## Make the end card belong

The logo and website get their own story at the end: a black card with light type. For the detail behind it, I went back to the brand audit and existing assets. The leaf artwork ties it to the rest of the brand without a new decorative language just for this video.

That took a few revisions. A visible square in the leaf treatment interrupted the background, so I changed the crop and mask. Then the leaves got too subtle in the encoded version, so I brought them back until they showed in the actual file.

The last product story fades to black before the end card arrives. I checked the joins in the exported sequence, and playback at desktop and phone sizes. A transition only counts if it survives the export people will watch.

## What does it still cost?

I sent the team one combined preview, with the individual files alongside. They could watch the pacing and review the pieces that would actually be delivered.

There will still be review, exceptions and occasional template changes, and rendering takes compute time. I haven't timed a live batch with this version yet. The aim is to avoid an extra editing pass for each drop, without claiming the work has no ongoing cost.

The earlier routine taught me to watch that cost. Idle days once got expensive because scheduled checks opened full agent sessions. Moving the first check into a script brought an idle day down to cents. That figure belongs to the old version.

## What's next

The style is set for the next flower drop. Next, the new renderer gets connected, so the same website media feeds the animated templates and the same review receives the output.
