For a couple of days in July, my automation's most expensive job was finding nothing to do.

It checked for a new product drop every hour from 3 to 9 a.m. Each check opened a full agent session, ran one shell script and stopped if there was no work. One idle day cost $12.10. It hadn't made a single video.

I still find that funny. But looking at the bill made me ask a useful question: how much of this routine actually needed a model?

## The mornings I was trying to get back

I lead design for three cannabis brands. Two or three times a week, the ecommerce team announces a flower drop: a few strains going live that morning, each needing a branded story card and a short vertical video.

I'd read the announcement, make an Asana task, find the product pages and download their photos and videos. In Figma, I'd duplicate the last card, swap the photo and retype the strain and tier. Then I'd convert the video in a terminal, replace the card and masked video in Premiere, and queue exports in Media Encoder.

After that came attaching files, handing off the posting work and sharing everything for approval in Slack. Seven tools, 3–5 hours a week. The steps were straightforward, but they kept landing on already busy mornings. The drop I used for the test run had gone out late because a client deadline hit that morning too.

## Work out the rules first

The handoff document had ten questions to answer before building: who owned the broadcast, which product page to use when there were duplicates, whether Premiere had to stay, and when people could be notified.

I worked through them with the agent. If it could look something up, like a channel ID or where a key lived, it fetched the answer for me to confirm. Each decision went into a Markdown run book.

The scheduled prompt only needed to say: check for work, and follow the run book if there is any. I wanted the rules somewhere I could read and change later.

## The wrong page looked good enough

Within the first hour, I asked for High Octane and got Gelato Fuel. A search fallback had found an unrelated strain, and nothing checked whether it matched.

Tropical Cookies went wrong another way. The scraper chose an older product page because the sitemap was four months stale and didn't include the new one.

I made the page selection more explicit. The scraper now tries likely product URLs directly, checks the title against the strain, ranks matches by tier and publication date, and records why it chose a page. That gave me something I could inspect when a choice looked wrong.

During that run, three review agents checked the scaffold against the interview notes. They found 26 issues, including two blockers. One was the wrong-page problem. The other was a state file that could mark a partially failed drop as handled and then skip it forever.

The routine now saves state after each side effect. It only marks a drop handled after delivery finishes.

## Figma got strange for a while

The cards use ITC Kabel, installed on my machine. Figma's cloud API could replace images and export frames, but it couldn't change text without access to the font.

I tried desktop automation. An invisible overlay left by a dictation app was eating clicks over the canvas. When the automation missed a text layer, the letters it typed became Figma shortcuts. “Illuminati” contains an `a`, so the frame tool came on and the following clicks drew giant frames over the artboards.

The script kept going as if it had changed the strain name.

Once I uploaded the font files to Figma, the API could edit the text. That took about two minutes. I deleted the desktop detour.

## Did this need Premiere?

When I looked closely at the template, it was a card with a product video masked into a circle. It ran for 4.73 seconds, with no animation or audio.

I took the circle's size and position from an old export and read the encoding settings from its metadata. One ffmpeg command could make the same story in seconds. It also removed a stray audio track that had survived the manual “no audio” exports.

A few weeks later, I removed the separate video conversion too. ffmpeg could read the source directly. The intermediate files had quietly taken up more than 5 GB in a synced folder.

## Making the files was only part of it

Products go live at 6 a.m. Eastern, or 3 a.m. for me. I wanted the routine to work before dawn without waking everyone else up.

Files can attach to Asana quietly, so that happens first. Comments and the Slack approval post wait until waking hours. The Slack connector couldn't attach files, so I used a small Slack app to upload the videos natively. They post as me and preview inline.

During the supervised trial, I checked the videos before any notifications went out.

## Then it posted the wrong tier

Two weeks in, a card reached the approval channel with the wrong tier.

The announcement said one thing and the product page said another. The new page used a URL pattern the scraper didn't know. The agent noticed the mismatch, checked the page's structured data, logged a “tier anomaly” and decided the website was right.

I had to delete an attachment, remove a Slack file by hand and post a correction. Before the approval post, remaking the card would have been easy. After it, other people were involved.

I made three changes. The announcement now decides the tier; the website only supplies media. A disagreement gets logged and never silently changes the category. The scraper also knows both URL patterns.

And if any strain is unfinished or uncertain, the whole drop waits. Finished files can attach quietly, but the approval post doesn't go out until everything is ready.

## Check at the right times, for less money

By late July, I wanted fewer scheduled runs. Before changing the schedule, I checked when announcements had actually arrived.

They ranged from 3:17 to 8:07 a.m. Pacific, mostly between 5:00 and 7:05. My planned cut would have missed three of eight drops. I was already catching half of them by running the routine myself around 8 a.m., after the schedule ended.

That brought me back to the $12.10 idle day. Every check opened a full agent session just to run a script. The task had also drifted onto a top-tier model, paying for tool definitions it rarely used.

I moved the first decision into a zsh script. It reads Slack and returns whether there's work, nothing to do, a completed day or an error. An error still sends the run onward so a broken token can't silently hide a drop.

A small model handles the dispatch. With no work, it prints a line and stops. With work, it passes the run book to a stronger model and relays the result. It doesn't retry a failure; I can pick that up myself instead of risking a half-finished post.

I kept three weekday checks at 3:30, 5:30 and 7:30 a.m. Pacific. After that, I'm awake. The split brought an idle day down to $0.71; three checks brought it to about $0.30. A drop day added a $6–8 pass taking 10–14 minutes.

Those numbers come from the agents' transcripts, read afterward. That's also how I found the model drift. The pipeline didn't need to report on itself, and the run book alone couldn't keep the model choice from changing.

## Where the July write-up left it

The run archive I reported from had 69 runs since July 9 and 37 drops delivered end to end, with no failed runs. Partial runs were the go-live guard waiting: a drop announced for tomorrow could get its task today, while the media waited for the product pages to go live. Those are the original report's figures, not a new October count.

I had gone from touching seven tools to checking the results. Each strain rendered in seconds rather than minutes. The routine used Claude agents, the zsh check, a zero-dependency TypeScript scraper, Figma components per tier, ffmpeg, Slack and Asana APIs, file-based state and a local HTML dashboard.

What I'd keep is the way the responsibilities shook out. Code can check a page title and render a circle. The model can handle the work that still needs interpretation. And before a result reaches other people, I want a clear reason to believe it's ready.

At the time of the write-up, I wanted to turn this into a video: the wrong scrape, the accidental frames, and the $12 “nothing to do.” Those moments explain the project better than a clean demo would.
