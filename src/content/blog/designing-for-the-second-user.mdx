Every onboarding flow I've designed assumed the same person: someone with an empty account and a few minutes. Ghosted's new walkthrough was built for exactly that person, and it shipped. The same evening I asked what it would do to Kim.

Kim was one of Ghosted's first testers. She already had a base resume, three jobs and applications out. She's the second user: the one who was here before the onboarding existed. The tour I'd just shipped was going to ask her to practice on her real work.

## The tour, as built for the first user

I wanted the ghost to hold your hand and explain things while trusting your intelligence, the way Adobe's hands-on tutorials and Logic's first run do. So the tour runs in the real app, not in a slideshow. The rest of the screen dims, the one control that matters sits in a cutout with a blue ring, and the ghost talks from a card beside it. Every step has Next and Dismiss, and doing the real thing counts as Next.

For a new account, that was nine steps, and most of them were hands-on:

1. Click a line on your base resume, and the edit form opens right there.
2. Ask the chat for a change.
3. Open New job, paste a link, and tailor your resume to it.
4. Review the first proposal: what the AI changed shows up in yellow, and you keep it or reject it.
5. Then the job's own copy, the Fit checklist and the Research tab.

That's great when there's nothing to break.

## What the tour would have done to Kim

I asked Claude, which builds Ghosted with me, what would happen to Kim's base resume and the jobs she'd already applied to. The first answer was reassuring and beside the point: the tour never changes anything by itself. Every change still goes through her own Keep, edit or tailor.

But that wasn't my question. A tour that asks you to practice is a tour that asks you to change things. For Kim, each hands-on step had a cost:

- **A practice edit on her base resume** would flag every one of her jobs with "Your base resume changed," because job copies watch the base for changes. That's a false alarm on each of her three jobs, including the ones she'd already applied with.
- **A new job** would add a fake application to her board.
- **Tailoring** would run an AI request she didn't need. Ghosted never makes AI calls you didn't ask for, and "the tour told me to" is a thin version of asking.
- **Leaving halfway** was its own problem. Press Next past "Tailor" and the tour waited for her next AI proposal. If that came days later, she'd get a "6 of 9" card about a tour she'd forgotten, on a change that had nothing to do with it.

None of this is a bug in the first-user design. It's the first-user design applied to someone it wasn't designed for.

## Three ways to handle her

I had three options:

1. **A sandbox:** run the hands-on tour on a sample resume, off to the side. Safe, but she'd learn the app on someone else's resume, and the sample would need cleaning up afterward.
2. **Keep it hands-on for everyone:** trust people to undo what they practiced. Fine on paper, but it's asking the people with the most to lose to do the most cleanup.
3. **Look, don't touch:** for accounts that already have jobs, the tour only explains. It shows the same places, but on her own work, and changes nothing.

I picked the third. The base steps keep their spotlight, but the copy changes from "try this" to "here's how." "Nothing to change now." Instead of New job, paste and tailor, the tour opens her most recent job and points at the parts that matter there. It was seven steps instead of nine, with no edits, no new job and no AI run she didn't start.

## When filling in is the right call

One exception made the look-don't-touch tour better than the hands-on one for her. Kim's jobs were older than the Fit report and the Research tab, so the tour would point at two empty tabs.

So when the tour opens a job that has no Fit report or research yet, it writes them as the job opens. That takes about 15 seconds, and they're ready by the time the tour gets there. It's the one place the tour makes an AI request, and I'm fine with it: taking the tour is the request. Showing someone an empty tab and calling it a feature is worse.

## Say it before they wonder

A careful tour doesn't help if people avoid it because they think it'll touch their work. Kim had every reason to think that. So the promise is in three places: the one-time offer ("It only points things out: nothing in it changes your work"), the first card ("Your resume and jobs stay exactly as they are: I'll only point things out"), and the What's new entry.

And a tour left waiting more than a day now ends quietly on the next load. You can replay it from the menu any time. A step popping up days later, out of context, is just noise.

## Then I made it shorter

A little later I asked whether the steps were as consolidated as they could be, and whether they covered what matters most. They weren't, and they didn't. Then I asked for it shorter still, and for an honest answer if it couldn't be.

- The first two steps spotlit the same thing, the resume page. Clicking it on step one already skipped step two, which tells you they were one idea.
- "This copy is the job's" repeated a line the job page already shows above the resume. It's now a sentence in the proposal card.
- Fit and Research each took two clicks: open the tab, then read it. They're one step now that points at both tabs, and each explains itself when you open it.
- And the tour stopped at Research, before the part the product is for. The last step is now "Send it": download the PDF, ask for the cover letter and answers, and mark the job Applied so Ghosted can nudge you if they go quiet.

That's seven steps for a new account and five for everyone else. What's left can't go without losing something: the resume page, the chat, New job (hidden behind a menu on phones), pasting the link, the first yellow proposal, and sending.

## What I'm taking to the next product

Three questions I'll ask about any onboarding from now on:

1. **What does each step write?** If a step changes data, it changes it for the person with the most data too.
2. **What happens if they leave halfway?** A tour is a state machine with no deadline. Decide when it expires.
3. **What do they already have?** The second user's own work is a better example than anything you'd stage. Point at it, and fill in what's missing instead of practicing on it.
